My approach

Build the structure around the leader.

Executive operations is not a collection of administrative tasks. It is the operating system around a decision-maker: what reaches them, what is ready before it reaches them, what moves without them, and what never gets dropped.

The premise

Attention is the scarce resource.

Most senior leaders do not need more information. They need the right information, in the right form, at the right moment. They do not need another person forwarding problems upward. They need someone who can sort, prepare, sequence, and close.

That is the work I have done across research, biotechnology, government, institutional development, and private real estate. The industries changed. The underlying function did not.

How I work

Five operating principles.

  • Protect the leader's attention.Not every issue deserves the same altitude. I separate what requires executive judgment from what can be resolved, prepared, or routed elsewhere.
  • Make decisions ready.A calendar invitation is not preparation. The question, context, options, constraints, and next action should be clear before the meeting begins.
  • Close the loop.Open matters create drag. I track commitments, unresolved questions, dependencies, and deadlines until there is an answer, a decision, or a documented next step.
  • Keep relationships warm.Operations runs through people. Attorneys, scientists, donors, contractors, tenants, public officials, vendors, and colleagues respond better when communication is clear, respectful, and consistent.
  • Build a system that survives urgency.Fast-moving environments cannot depend on memory. I create practical structures for calendars, documents, communications, decisions, finances, and follow-through so the work remains visible under pressure.

The method

Find out what is true. Then make it usable.

My scientific training shaped the way I approach operations: define the question, gather what is observable, separate fact from assumption, and resist the conclusion you were hoping to find.

That method shows up in very practical ways. It means reconstructing a chain of title before an acquisition decision. It means finding the real constraint in a stalled project. It means preparing a briefing that distinguishes what is known, what is disputed, and what requires professional judgment. It means telling the leader the truth early enough to do something useful with it.

The goal is not to be indispensable because nothing can function without me. The goal is to make the operation dependable because the right structure exists.

What this looks like in practice

Different settings. Same operating discipline.

At Merck, it meant helping stand up a new research facility while coordinating calendars, international travel, budgets, procurement, scientists, facilities, IT, AV, security, vendors, and visitors.

At Genzyme, it meant supporting a division president and the senior executive leading business development during Sanofi's proposed acquisition of the company, while keeping executive reports, budgets, vendors, recruiting, and external affiliations moving.

In public service, it meant helping establish the operating infrastructure of newly elected offices, from communications and constituent systems to official mailings, stakeholder relationships, and leadership briefings.

In private development, it means bringing structure to property acquisition, contracts, due diligence, financial administration, communications, and decisions across concurrent multimillion-dollar projects.

Building something complex?

I work best with leaders who are moving quickly, carrying too much, and ready for the operation around them to become more deliberate.
